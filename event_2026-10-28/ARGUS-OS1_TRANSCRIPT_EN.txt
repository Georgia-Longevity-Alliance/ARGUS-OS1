[02] The Problem
Start with the problem. Aging research depends on watching living cells over long periods: division rates, lineage structure, senescence, and the slow drift of a culture toward its replicative limit. To study any of these you need to observe the same cells continuously, ideally for days at a stretch, and to know, at every moment, which cell is which. The instruments available today fail this requirement in one of three ways. Commercial systems, in the class produced by the major microscopy vendors, give you excellent optics and tightly controlled environments. They hold focus, they regulate carbon dioxide and temperature, and they can image for days. But they are closed. The firmware, the software, and the analysis are proprietary. The data move through vendor pipelines, and a capable configuration costs well over a hundred thousand dollars. A laboratory with a modest budget cannot buy one, and a laboratory that can buy one still cannot inspect what it does. Open hardware, led by projects like the OpenFlexure microscope, solves the cost and the openness. You get a fully open instrument for about a thousand dollars, with every file published. But the OpenFlexure design images on a fixed schedule and leaves all analysis to you, after the fact. It is an open microscope, not an open pipeline. The instrument does not decide anything, and the biology that accumulates between acquisitions is lost. Cloud AI pipelines close the analysis gap in the other direction. They are computationally strong, and they handle segmentation and tracking at scale. But your raw biology leaves the building. Every frame costs money, every frame needs a network call, and a long experiment becomes a continuous billing event. Worse, the model that runs in the cloud is not yours to inspect, and the raw data no longer live on your hardware. None of these close the loop on the instrument itself. What we wanted was a microscope that acquires, segments, tracks, and then decides what to image next, on the device, with zero network dependency, and with every layer open. That loop, closed on physically-owned hardware, is the gap that ARGUS-OS1 was built to fill, and everything in this talk returns to it.

[03] Why C. elegans
Now the model system. We chose the roundworm, Caenorhabditis elegans, and the reason matters to anyone building an automated imaging pipeline: it is the best-mapped organism in biology. The complete cell lineage is known. The embryonic lineage was traced cell by cell by Sulston and colleagues, published in the early nineteen eighties, and the post-embryonic lineage, which accounts for the nine hundred and fifty-nine cells of the adult hermaphrodite, was published by Sulston and Horvitz in nineteen seventy-seven. Every division axis is documented. That means the geometry of any division is defined objectively, in the coordinates of the animal, and not guessed from an image. The fate of every cell is known in advance, so a machine can predict a fate and then verify it. For our specific question, the relevant fact is that during embryogenesis about eighty-eight percent of somatic cells eliminate their centrioles. This was shown by Kalbfuss and Goenczy in two thousand twenty-three, in a study titled extensive programmed centriole elimination in C. elegans embryos. Only a minority of cells keep their centrioles, and that asymmetry is exactly the variation we want to explain. Why does the full map matter for AI? Ground truth. When a segmentation algorithm makes an error, you can check it against a published lineage map. When a tracker loses a cell, you know which cell it should have been following. When a division event is missed, the map tells you it was missed. That verifiability is the difference between an automated system you trust and an automated system you merely run. A second, more subtle point. Because every division axis is documented, we can define a geometry of inheritance: for each centriole, a sequence of division orientations stretching back toward the zygote. The worm gives us a system where that history is knowable, computable, and testable, which is precisely what a hypothesis about centriole pedigree requires. No other model organism offers this combination of complete lineage, known fates, and a fixed, documented geometry of division.

[04] The Hypothesis
Now the scientific question, stated concretely. A centriole is a small cylindrical organelle, about two hundred to two hundred and fifty nanometres across, that organizes the microtubule cytoskeleton and, during division, the mitotic spindle. Centrioles are inherited with history. Each division hands one centriole to each daughter cell, and over the generations each centriole carries a record of every division it has passed through. In the worm, most cells eliminate their centrioles during embryogenesis, and a minority keep them. Our question is whether the division history of a centriole, captured as the sequence of division orientations, predicts whether it is kept or eliminated. We condense that history into a single number, the Pedigree Score, built from five geometric metrics. The first is the fraction of divisions parallel to the body axis. The second is the mean change in three-dimensional angle between consecutive divisions. The third is the variance of those angles. The fourth is the number of orientation switches, transitions between parallel and perpendicular. The fifth is the cumulative angular path, the total angular distance a lineage has travelled. These five are combined into a principal component, computed and fixed before the data collection, so the score is pre-registered and cannot be tuned to fit the result. The cleanest test is the sister-cell pair. When a cell divides, the two daughters inherit the same cell type, the same cytoplasm, and the same lineage history. The only systematic difference between them is which centriole each one receives. If the pedigree of the centriole matters, sister pairs will diverge in a way that is directly attributable to geometry, with no confounding by cell type or position. This is the design that gives the experiment its power. There is also a longevity thread, and a point that matters because it explains why a centriole-tracking platform belongs in longevity science at all. Centriole homeostasis connects to signalling that converges on the transcription factor DAF-16, the master output of the insulin and IGF-1 pathway, and to the heat-shock axis, where the prefoldin subunit PFD-6 relays HSF-1 to DAF-16, as Son and colleagues showed in twenty eighteen. If centriole dynamics respond to the same signalling that sets lifespan, then a centriole is more than an organelle; it is a readout. The platform we are building tonight is the instrument that can measure that readout, in single cells, over long times, in a way that is open and reproducible.

[05] Hardware Build
Now the build, and we start with hardware, because the honesty of the whole project begins in the bill of materials. The mechanics come from the OpenFlexure microscope, an open, three-dimensionally printed instrument with motorized stage and focus. We use the current version of the design, printed in engineering plastics, driven by the standard Sangaboard stepper drivers. The objective is a forty-times, zero point seven five numerical aperture, dry lens. That gives the resolution we need for the early experiments, imaging cells and centriole-scale signals, without the cost and complexity of immersion optics. The camera is the Raspberry Pi HQ module, with its twelve megapixel IMX477 sensor and a C-mount adapter, which is the standard imaging path in the OpenFlexure ecosystem. Compute sits on the instrument. The module is an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX, drawing on the order of five to fifteen watts, which runs the entire vision stack on-device. This is the deliberate choice: a small, power-efficient edge processor instead of a cloud GPU, so that the loop stays local. The sample environment is a HEPA H13 filtered incubator, with a PID temperature controller running on a Raspberry Pi Pico, feedback from a PT1000 probe, a solid-state relay driving a resistive heater, and an acrylic enclosure around the sample plane. Power comes from twelve and twenty-four volt supplies, with the usual wiring and printed circuit board work. The complete bill of materials, line by line, with suppliers, lives in the repository. It comes to two thousand, nine hundred and ninety-six dollars, roughly three thousand. I want to pause on that document, because it is the point: this is a reproducible instrument, not a one-off custom build. Any group can order these parts, print the frame, and build the same machine. Everything is printable, everything is replaceable, nothing is proprietary. Two engineering details deserve a closer look. First, the motor-release function. When the incubator heats up, the stage can bind from thermal expansion. We found that the motor firmware already supported releasing the motors, so we exposed that function through the microscope server, and solved a thermal problem in software instead of hardware. Small detail, large effect on reliability. Second, and this is our main gap, holding focus over days. We benchmarked our platform against a commercial instrument with infrared hardware autofocus, and the conclusion was unambiguous: focus-hold is the single most important engineering problem for long-term imaging. Without it, multi-day acquisition drifts out of focus and the run is lost. We are prototyping an affordable fiducial-marker autofocus on the OpenFlexure frame, following a recent method from the microscopy literature that uses small fiducial markers for reliable long-term autofocus. This is where the engineering effort is going, and it is the honest place to put it.

[06] Edge AI Pipeline
Now the brain of the instrument. Everything we just listed exists to feed a closed software loop that runs on the Jetson with no network. The loop has four steps, and I will take them one at a time. Acquire. The camera takes images on a schedule that the instrument itself decides. This is not a fixed interval; the schedule is a biological decision, and I will come back to it. Segment. A deep-learning model, CellPose version two, detects cells and the small centriole signals within them. The model is optimized for the edge, compiled with TensorRT at reduced precision, so it runs at interactive speed on a five to fifteen watt module. Segmentation is the place where raw pixels become objects, and where the quality of everything downstream is decided. Track. A Bayesian lineage tracker reconstructs the division tree frame by frame. The word Bayesian does the work. Instead of committing to a single guess about which cell is which, the tracker maintains a distribution over hypotheses, and carries its uncertainty forward through time. When a decision is ambiguous, the system knows it is ambiguous, and it can ask for more data rather than force an answer. This is the difference between a tracker and a greedy assignment algorithm. Schedule. This is the step that makes the device a microscope rather than a camera. The instrument decides what to image next based on the biological state it observes. A cell approaching division is imaged more often, because that is where the information is. A quiet field is left alone, because imaging it costs light and time. The instrument spends its light budget where it buys the most information. That is biological scheduling. Now, what do we mean by age-related readouts, since the title of the evening mentions longevity? Concretely: division-rate dynamics across the lineage, sister-cell pair comparisons, and, in the next stage, replicative senescence kinetics, the Hayflick limit, in immortalized fibroblasts. All of this is computed locally. The design rule is simple, and I will state it twice. The core loop, segment, track, schedule, log, is one hundred percent local and one hundred percent inspectable. Nothing about the experiment depends on an external service. Every decision is logged with its input frames and its model outputs, so any result can be audited against the raw record. If a paper is later written from this instrument, the reader can trace every number back to the images and the decisions that produced it.

[07] Local Compute & DAIS
This is the principle that ties the design together, and it deserves its own section. Your raw frames never leave the instrument. Images and derived features stay on hardware you physically own, which gives you data locality by architecture. There is no account to create, no API to pay for, no bandwidth to consume, and no third party that can see your data. An experiment can run unattended for days, and, with the servicing layer we will discuss shortly, for months. On top of that we built a verifiable AI layer called DAIS. The purpose is to make autonomous decisions auditable, the way a flight recorder makes an aircraft auditable. Every autonomous body gets a passport. Every intervention leaves a trace. Every knowledge claim carries a provenance, and the status is explicit: proposed is not fact. A claim stays a claim until it is backed by a source, by evidence, and by a replication record. Two principles keep this safe, and they are the reason we can put a language model on an instrument that touches living cells. The first: safety-critical steps, firmware, stage control, graph traversal, stay deterministic. They are not delegated to a model. The second: language models are confined to well-defined lanes, anomaly diagnosis, log mining, procedure lookup. Nothing safety-relevant is decided by a neural network. The machine can plan, but it cannot override the physics. The practical consequence is that you get the flexibility of a learned system and the reliability of a deterministic one. That combination is unusual in microscopy, and it is the reason we can propose long, unattended runs at all. When the platform says it detected a laser power spike and corrected it, you can inspect the trace, see the threshold, see the action, and verify it, because the record is part of the instrument, not a marketing claim.

[08] The Differentiator
Now the differentiator, and this is the heart of the argument. Every commercial live-cell imaging system is built for hours to days of observation, and assumes you will open the incubator when you need to act. ARGUS is built for months. Two walls stop the commercial systems, and we designed past both. The first wall is light. A long-term experiment is a light-budget problem. Every excitation pulse damages the sample. To be blunt: even transmitted brightfield light measurably kills cells over days, as shown in a recent study on ovarian follicles, and fluorescence adds more damage on top of that. The longer the run, the tighter the budget, and commercial systems illuminate continuously, on fixed schedules, without regard for how much light a given cell has already received. We attack phototoxicity by architecture, in five moves. Multi-channel LED illumination instead of broad sources, so the spectrum is matched to the fluorophore and nothing else. Image-on-demand scheduling, so light is spent only when it buys information. A calibrated label-free channel, which assigns old and new centrioles from morphology where staining is unnecessary, saving fluorescence entirely. At the OS3 stage, light-sheet illumination, which excites only the focal plane and cuts phototoxicity roughly ten-fold on thick samples. And at OS4, optoelectronic tweezers, which manipulate cells with light-patterned electric fields at about one hundred thousand times lower light intensity than optical tweezers. Finally, we co-report the illumination dose with every frame, so phototoxicity becomes auditable instead of assumed. The light budget is a measured quantity, logged alongside the images, not a guess. The second wall is service. A culture that runs for months needs servicing. Medium runs out. Cells overgrow. Samples must be added and removed. Commercial systems cannot do any of this without breaking the run, because opening the incubator breaks the environment and the schedule. ARGUS has a transfer box with two doors, a hardware interlock, ultraviolet sterilization, and a HEPA purge, so material enters and leaves sterilely without opening the enclosure. It has glove ports, so a human can reach in and work. And the V9 layer adds cable-driven robot arms that operate through those ports twenty-four seven: sample exchange, medium replenishment, objective cleaning, and more. Inside the culture, micro-bots and micro-tweezers manipulate individual cells without breaking sterility. No commercial vendor offers autonomous sample change. That is not an exaggeration, it is a gap in the market. The combination, minimal phototoxicity plus a closed serviceable box, is what makes multi-month observation physically possible. That is the differentiator, and it is why this platform is not a cheaper version of a commercial system. It is a different capability.

[10] Lessons
What has running AI-first biology on owned hardware actually taught us? Four lessons, stated as honestly as we can. First, the software loop is easier than the biology. Getting segmentation and tracking to run on a Jetson is, in large part, a solved problem. There are good models, good libraries, and good practice. Keeping a sample alive and in focus for days is not solved. It is biology plus thermal engineering plus fluidics plus patience. Focus-hold is our number-one gap. When we benchmarked against a commercial system with infrared hardware autofocus, the delta was unmistakable, and we are closing it with an open fiducial-marker autofocus on the OpenFlexure frame. Second, open hardware is not an open pipeline. OpenFlexure gives you the mechanics, and that is real value. But the acquisition-to-analysis loop was still a separate, closed step. The instrument took pictures; the analysis happened later, on a different machine, with different software. Closing that loop on-device, making the instrument decide what to image and what to ignore, was the actual engineering work. It is easy to underestimate how much of the project lives in that seam. Third, local language models are good enough for the boring ninety percent. Anomaly diagnosis, log mining, procedure lookup: these are the tasks that keep an unattended experiment healthy, and a small local model handles them well. Keeping them out of the safety-critical ten percent is not a compromise; it is the design. Fourth, a benchmark you can afford changes the conversation. We ran the numbers against a hundred-thousand-dollar commercial class instrument, and we found the gaps are closable with open solutions. And we add one thing the commercial systems do not have: autonomous sample change. I want to be precise about this. That is not a cheaper version of the same thing. It is a different capability, one that no vendor currently sells, and it is the capability that makes months-long autonomous observation possible. If I have one takeaway from this section, it is that the hard problems in this field are not the ones people advertise. They are the unglamorous ones, focus, environment, servicing, and light budget, and an open project can name them publicly and work on them in the open.

[11] Roadmap OS1-OS4
Now the roadmap, and here the talk opens out, because the instrument you have seen tonight is the first rung of a ladder that reaches far beyond it. It is one platform in four stages, designed so that each stage is buildable and benchmarkable on its own, and each stage answers a different kind of question. OS1 is what we have shown tonight: the OpenFlexure frame, a forty-times dry objective, a Jetson, and a closed on-device loop, at roughly three to five thousand dollars. Its job is to prove the closed loop, to generate the first lineage data, and to give the community a machine that any laboratory can afford. OS1 answers a correlation question: does centriole pedigree correlate with fate? OS2 is the core science stage. The objective moves to water immersion, a sixty-times, one point two numerical aperture lens, which brings the image to the diffraction limit of living cells. The camera becomes an sCMOS, in the class used by commercial systems, which captures the faint centriole signals with low noise and at speed. We add phase contrast for label-free morphology, a Jetson AGX for the heavier tracking workload, and microfluidics for loading and unloading embryos without disturbing the culture. This is the instrument that runs the hundred-embryo pedigree experiment, roughly six thousand eight hundred centrioles after exclusions, with sister-cell pairs as the primary comparison. OS2 answers a prediction question: does pedigree predict fate better than position, cell type, or age? The hardware for OS2 is on the order of seventy thousand dollars, and the full project, including personnel and biology, is several times that. OS3 is the causality stage. It adds two capabilities that change what the platform can ask. First, light-sheet illumination, which excites only the focal plane and cuts phototoxicity roughly ten-fold on thick samples, so we can watch development over longer windows. Second, a femtosecond laser for precise subcellular ablation, in a closed loop: detect a cell, classify it, mask the targets that must survive, irradiate, and verify the outcome in the next frames. This turns the machine from an observer into an experimenter. The scientific target is a causality test: we can remove a centriole or transplant one, and ask whether changing the centriole changes the fate. The broader program, which we call the entropy reset protocol, borrows from what nature does between meiosis and the first blastomeres: centriole elimination followed by de novo synthesis, but without loss of the diploid genome. If a change of the centriole changes the trajectory of a somatic cell, that is a causal link from the organelle to cellular aging. OS3 also carries the full robotic servicing layer: micromanipulators and micro-bots inside the enclosure, working on individual cells. OS4 reworks the geometry. The microscope becomes inverted, objective below the sample, and the specimen sits on a thin hundred-and-seventy-micrometre coverslip. That single change unlocks numerical aperture up to one point three five, because the high-aperture water-immersion objective can now work within its working distance. And OS4 adds a light-driven manipulation layer: optoelectronic tweezers. A light pattern switches local conductivity on a photoconductive surface, which creates virtual electrodes that push and pull cells with electric fields. The key number: about one hundred thousand times lower light intensity than optical tweezers, so cells can be moved, sorted, and delivered contact-free, without photodamage. Up to fifteen thousand parallel traps can be drawn with a consumer projector. Around this, a family of soft micro-robots, tiny grippers printed by two-photon polymerization, can hold, rotate, and kiss single cells. This is where the micro-bots and micro-tweezers we mentioned earlier actually live. Across every stage, the V9 autonomy layer adds robotic hands and a shared local LLM brain, turning the platform into a self-servicing laboratory. This is the layer that makes months of unattended operation a design feature rather than a hope. On funding: an Open Source Collective application is under review. An NLnet open call has been submitted. And a Foresight call for AI-first biology is drafted for the October thirty-first deadline. One last point about the ladder. Each stage is designed to be built and benchmarked by the community. We are not asking anyone to trust the full pyramid at once. We are asking people to build the first stage and check it. The cheapest rung costs three thousand dollars, and the scientific question at the top, whether changing a centriole changes a cell's fate, is one of the cleanest causal questions in aging biology.
